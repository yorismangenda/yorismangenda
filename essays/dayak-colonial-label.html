The Afterlives of a Colonial Label: The Epistemic Politics of “Dayak” in Postcolonial Borneo

Yoris Mangenda · 2026

BODY TEXT ENDNOTES REFERENCES

Abstract

This paper examines how the colonial label “Dayak” continues to shape the production of identity and governance in postcolonial Borneo. Rather than a neutral ethnonym, “Dayak” originated as a classificatory device that rendered interior populations intelligible through hierarchical regimes of representation established under colonial rule.

The study traces how these epistemic formations were reorganised—rather than dismantled—after independence, informing Indonesia’s “developmental assimilation,” Malaysia’s “redistributive hierarchy” of indigeneity, and Brunei’s “cultural homogenisation.” These state practices relied on techniques that made interior communities administratively visible and governable.

Yet the endurance of these structures also generated conditions for new forms of Dayak self-articulation, in which communities negotiate, appropriate, or subvert imposed classifications. The paper argues that “Dayak” functions not as a fixed identity but as a contested terrain where colonial legacies, state rationalities, and Indigenous agency continually intersect.

Introduction

Borneo, the world’s third-largest island, stands as a testament to the enduring intricacies of colonial power and ethnic identity. In the nineteenth century, the island was divided among three colonial powers: the central and southern regions under the Dutch East Indies; the northern territories governed by the Brooke Raj in Sarawak and the British North Borneo Company in Sabah, while the coastal area remained under the Sultanate of Brunei as a British protectorate.

These colonial legacies not only produced the administrative and political boundaries that later became Indonesia, Malaysia, and Brunei Darussalam, but also laid the epistemic foundations that shaped how colonial knowledge production mapped, classified, and shaped the understanding of the inhabitants of Borneo.

In colonial administrative archives, Borneo’s demographic landscape was generally reduced or simplified into a basic dichotomy: “Malay” for Muslim populations living in the “coastal” areas and “Dayak” for non-Muslim populations living in the “interior.” This dichotomy was never a neutral classification—particularly regarding the label “Dayak.” Although both categories were products of colonial reduction, “Dayak” covered dozens, even hundreds, of ethnic groups that differed in their cultures, social structures, and languages. 1

Colonial authorities used the label “Dayak” as both an administrative tool and an epistemic device to manage diverse interior populations, bolstering moral and civilisational hierarchies. The construction of tribal identities in Borneo was tied to qualities such as “civilised,” “wild and warlike,” “hierarchical,” and “untrustworthy.” 2

Although formal colonial rule in Borneo ended, the epistemic structures and stereotypes that shaped understandings of the “Dayak” did not disappear. Colonial classifications continued to influence how interior communities were represented and governed in the postcolonial period. 3

Genealogies of the “Dayak”: Labels, Stereotypes, and the Construction of Difference

The term “Dayak” was not an endogenous identity or a self-ascribed category, but an epistemic legacy that emerged from colonial practices of knowledge production. Its administrative use developed through British and Dutch colonial accounts, where it helped distinguish diverse interior populations from coastal Malay communities.

Through colonial systems of representation, heterogeneous populations were transformed into a single category of knowledge. These classificatory acts reinforced unequal relations between colonisers and colonised populations.

The Coloniality of Knowledge and the Postcolonial Reproduction of “Dayak”

Despite the end of formal colonial rule, the epistemic foundations surrounding the label “Dayak” persisted. Stereotypes attached during the colonial period were reworked into representational frameworks that allowed postcolonial administrative and academic authorities to classify and understand interior populations. 4

The Governance of Difference: Institutionalising the “Dayak” in Postcolonial States

In Indonesia, Malaysia, and Brunei Darussalam, colonial classificatory logic became embedded in institutional policies. These policies shaped how interior populations were administered, categorised, and incorporated into national projects.

Rearticulating the “Dayak”: Negotiation, Hybridity, and Cultural Reclaiming

The rearticulation of “Dayak” identity involves communities negotiating their cultural identities amid changing political landscapes. Contemporary articulations do not produce a single, uniform identity, but reflect different histories, political configurations, and forms of local agency. 5

Conclusion

This paper reveals that the term “Dayak” is a powerful colonial construct that continues to influence perceptions and governance of Indigenous identity in Borneo. The classificatory logic did not disappear with decolonisation; it transformed into forms of state rationality that continue to shape how Dayak communities are recognised and governed.

Ultimately, “Dayak” represents an epistemic battleground where colonial legacies, state power, and Indigenous rearticulation converge. Viewing “Dayak” as a formation that is constructed and subsequently reclaimed, rather than a stable identity, offers a framework for understanding indigeneity in postcolonial Borneo.
