This paper examines how the colonial label “Dayak” continues to shape the production of identity and governance in postcolonial Borneo. Rather than a neutral ethnonym, “Dayak” originated as a classificatory device that rendered interior populations intelligible through hierarchical regimes of representation established under colonial rule. The study traces how these epistemic formations were reorganised–rather than dismantled–after independence, informing Indonesia’s “developmental assimilation”, Malaysia’s “redistributive hierarchy” of indigeneity, and Brunei’s “cultural homogenisation.” These state practices relied on techniques that made interior communities administratively visible and governable, turning inherited stereotypes and typologies into actionable policy categories. Yet the endurance of these structures also generated conditions for new forms of Dayak self-articulation, in which communities negotiate, appropriate, or subvert imposed classifications. The paper argues that “Dayak” functions not as a fixed identity but as a contested terrain where colonial legacies, state rationalities, and indigenous agency continually intersect. Introduction Borneo, the world’s third-largest island, stands as a testament to the enduring intricacies of colonial power and ethnic identity. In the nineteenth century, the island was divided among three colonial powers: the central and southern regions under the Dutch East Indies; the northern territories governed by the Brooke Raj in Sarawak and the British North Borneo Company in Sabah, while the coastal area remained under the Sultanate of Brunei as a British protectorate. These colonial legacies not only produced the administrative and political boundaries that later became Indonesia, Malaysia, and Brunei Darussalam, but also laid the epistemic foundations that shaped how colonial knowledge production mapped, classified, and shaped the understanding of the inhabitants of Borneo.

In colonial administrative archives, Borneo’s demographic landscape was generally reduced or simplified into a basic dichotomy: “Malay” for Muslim populations living in the “coastal” areas and “Dayak” for non-Muslim populations living in the “interior.” This dichotomy was never a neutral classification—particularly regarding the label “Dayak.” Although both the Malay–Dayak categories were products of colonial reduction, Sellato (1994) notes that “Dayak” covers dozens—even hundreds—of ethnic groups that differ in their cultures, social structures, and languages.1 King (1993) further argues that the term reflected colonial convenience rather than real similarities in the social, cultural, or religious practices among these diverse populations.2

Colonial authorities used the label “Dayak” as both an administrative tool and an epistemic device to manage the diverse interior populations, bolstering moral and civilizational hierarchies. As Cleary (1996) observes, the construction of tribal identities in Borneo was tied to qualities such as “civilised,” “wild and warlike,” “hierarchical,” and “untrustworthy,” which justified colonial policies and practices.3 This produced an imbalance in the Malay–Dayak dichotomy, where coastal Malays were associated with being “civilised,” while interior communities classified as Dayak were framed as “tribal.” This dynamic shows that “Dayak” was not an endogenous identity but a colonial construction that turned diversity into a governable category.

The epistemic foundations of “Dayak” were reinforced not only through administrative practice but also through academic apparatuses. From the mid-nineteenth to the mid-twentieth century, stereotypes such as “wild,” “headhunting,” “primitive,” and “animistic” were consistently attached and reinforced through ethnographic writings such as those by Hugh Low (1848), Hose and McDougall (1921), as well as through other scientific documentation. These representational practices were not merely descriptive; they were integral to the colonial mechanism that legitimised moral hierarchies by positioning the Dayak as subjects to be governed. This dynamic reflects what Said (1978) calls “colonial discourse,” where acts of representation are never neutral in producing and constructing “the Other” for the interests of colonial authority.4

Although formal colonial rule in Borneo ended with Indonesia’s independence in 1945, Malaysia’s in 1957, and Brunei Darussalam’s in 1984, the epistemic structures and stereotypes that shaped understandings of the “Dayak” did not disappear. As a result, ways of seeing the “Dayak,” and the ways in which they were governed in postcolonial administrations, continued to operate within similar epistemic formulations expressed in more contemporary vocabularies.

The reproduction of these epistemic structures did not occur only within postcolonial state institutions; it also operated through academic knowledge production that sustained these frameworks. As Zawawi (2017) notes, postcolonial anthropological works such as Geddes (1954) and Harrisson (1970) positioned the Dayak as researchers interpreting their own experiences rather than as subjects who are interpreted from the outside—establishing the “knowledge base” through which they would be studied.6 This simultaneity between state discourse and academic discourse reflects Michel Foucault’s (1977) formulation of “power/knowledge,” wherein power not only regulates but also produces knowledge, and both work together to produce subjects who can then be classified and explained.7

Although colonial epistemic structures strongly influenced the postcolonial era and shaped contemporary Dayak identity, many studies still focus on cultural and linguistic diversity, often neglecting to map the concrete effects or transformations of these legacies. This paper therefore emerges from a central question: How does a colonial label—originally used to classify, stigmatise, and govern—persist and even reactivate as an identity in the postcolonial states of Borneo?

To explore this question, the paper employs four analytical approaches, each offering a unique lens to understand the persistence and transformation of the “Dayak” identity. Genealogies of the “Dayak”: Labels, Stereotypes, and the Construction of Difference The term “Dayak” was not an endogenous identity or a self-ascribed category, but an epistemic legacy that emerged from colonial practices of knowledge production. The term “Dyaks” appeared in John Hunt’s early nineteenth-century reports to Stamford Raffles to describe interior populations of Borneo, later published in “Sketch of Borneo” (1812-1820). Its administrative use by the British was consolidated through James Brooke’s expeditions in the late 1830s and became further formalised when Brooke became Rajah of Sarawak in 1841. Around the same period, Dutch East Indies administrators also adopted the terms “Dajaks” and “Dajakkers” in administrative documents such as the “Koloniaal Verslag” and “Regeerings-Almanak voor Nederlandsch-Indië,” indicating the spread and consistency of this vocabulary across colonial bureaucracies. Within the “representational” logic of colonialism, these early usages functioned as administrative tools for European mapping practices to distinguish between the diverse “interior” tribes and the Malay “coastal” civilisation.

Following Edward Said’s (1978) argument, racial labeling in the colonial context was never a neutral descriptive act but the initial step in the Orientalist project of stabilising “the Other.”8 By establishing terms like “Dyaks–Dajaks–Dajakkers,” colonial administrators transformed the diversity of Borneo’s interior populations into a single, governable unit of knowledge. This imposed a coherence that had not previously existed and reinforced unequal power relations between the “coloniser” and the “colonised.” Such classificatory acts demonstrate what Said describes as the “strategic location” of the observer–a position of superiority that granted colonial authorities the power to define, categorise, and rank colonised populations within a system of epistemic and political domination.9 Thus, the emergence of the term “Dayak” did not reflect the interior communities’ self-understanding; rather, colonial authorities produced it as a representation–an identity created for administrative and intellectual purposes, not grounded in the lived realities of those being labeled.

From the mid to late nineteenth century, the term “Dayak” expanded beyond administrative usage and entered more systematic domains such as linguistics and ethnography. T. Hardeland’s “Dajacksch–Deutsches Wörterbuch” (1859) was one of the earliest attempts to codify a “Dajacksch” language, embedding the term within classificatory frameworks that linked language, race, and culture. Spencer St. John’s “Life in the Forests of the Far East” (1862) also contributed to stabilising “Dayak” as an ethnographic category by attributing certain social and moral “traits” to interior groups. These works effectively shifted “Dayak” from a bureaucratic term to a fixed “scientific” category—an epistemic instrument that brought race, culture, and geography together within the classificatory logic of colonial modernity. 9 Ibid., 20. 8 Edward W. Etymologically, the term “Dayak” does not originate from a clear linguistic root and remains the subject of academic debate. King (1993) suggests that the term may derive from the Malay root “aja,” interpreted as “native” or “inlander,” reflecting distinctions by coastal Malays toward non-Muslim interior groups.10 Yet, whichever linguistic origin is assumed, those later grouped under the label “Dayak” never used the term to identify themselves. Instead, they referred to toponyms, river systems, or linguistic affiliations–such as Iban, Taman, Meratus, Ahe, and others. Despite this ambiguity, colonial administrators institutionalised the term to stabilise perceived differences between interior populations and coastal Malay groups. As Sellato (1994) emphasises, what came to be grouped under “Dayak” consisted of dozens–even hundreds–of communities, each with its own social organisation, language, and worldview.11

Over time, people increasingly attached the term “Dayak,” initially applied simply to denote “interior” or “non-Muslim,” to negative stereotypes and stigma. Moral and civilisational hierarchies were constructed through both administrative and exploratory practices as well as nineteenth- and early twentieth-century ethnographic and scientific writings. Hugh Low’s “Sarawak: Its Inhabitants and Productions” (1848), for example, portrayed the “Dyaks” as drunken, debauched headhunters engaged in “disgusting rituals,” reflecting sensationalist and moralistic European representations of interior Bornean communities.12 Several decades later, Charles Hose and William McDougall reinforced these depictions in “The Pagan Tribes of Borneo” (1912), consistently using terms such as “pagan,” “wild,” “primitive,” “headhunting,” and “animistic” to describe the “Dyaks.13” These portrayals contributed to a symbolic order in which “Dayak” became the antonym of “civilised” and “modern” within European imaginaries.

In the late colonial period, visual practices further reinforced these hierarchies. Protschky (2015), in her study of G. L. Tichelman’s photographic documentation of Queen Wilhelmina’s birthday celebrations, shows that the colonial visual narratives of modernity excluded the Dayak—who were perceived as “primitive” and “endangered.”14 Her concept of a “regime of (in)visibility” captures how colonial power not only represented subjects selectively but also strategically erased them from its symbolic order. As Maunati (2004) notes, representations of the Dayak as “head-hunters” continued to be perpetuated through works such as Carl Brooke’s “The Head-Hunters of Borneo” (1881), Charles C. Examining these historiographic traces makes clear that colonial authorities established an initial epistemic framework intended to manage the diversity of Borneo’s interior populations. This framework was reinforced by the persistent construction of stereotypes throughout the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, giving disproportionate moral weight to the label. This dynamic reflects Said’s (1978) concept of Orientalism: a “style of thought” based on a distinction between the West and those marked as “the Other,” sustained through institutions, vocabularies, bureaucracies, and visual representation.16 The repeated portrayals of the Dayak as “wild,” “pagan,” “head-hunting,” “animistic,” and “drunken” by Low, Hose, and McDougall, Carl Brooke, and many others constituted part of the production of a subject of difference–fortifying the epistemic foundations of the Dayak.

Collectively, these textual and visual practices reproduced what Said (1978) describes as a “system of representation”–a discursive field in which the Dayak were made highly visible as ethnographic objects but invisible as historical agents.17 These descriptions did not function as neutral representations but as techniques of power: by stabilising the image of the Dayak as primitive, colonial discourse legitimised its authority to intervene and govern. This representational foundation endured far beyond the colonial period and shaped the epistemic assumptions inherited by postcolonial states, so that ways of seeing–and governing–the Dayak continued to follow similar patterns even after formal decolonisation.

Through this perspective, the genealogy of the term “Dayak” reveals not only a series of labels and stereotypes but also the gradual construction of a representational regime that made interior populations governable. The classificatory and representational logics that transformed heterogeneous communities into a single object of knowledge became the conceptual infrastructure upon which later institutions–from ethnography to state administration–continued to define, categorise, and control “Dayak.” Colonial discourse thus produced what may be described as a “regime of truth” that persisted after decolonisation. Instead, colonialism generated a coloniality that persisted, reappearing in softened vocabularies, being adopted into bureaucratic categories, and continuing through academic practices that shape how “Dayak” are known, represented, and governed.

The Coloniality of Knowledge and the Postcolonial Reproduction of “Dayak” Despite the end of formal colonial rule in Borneo, the entrenched epistemic foundations surrounding the label ‘Dayak’ persisted beyond Indonesia’s independence in 1945, Malaysia’s in 1958, and Brunei Darussalam’s in 1984. Stereotypes attached during the colonial period, such as “primitive,” “inlander,” “headhunter,” and “animistic,” did not 17 Ibid., 21. 16 Edward W. The classificatory logic inherited from colonialism persisted–reframed within softer vocabularies of development or culture. This framework continued to provide the basis for postcolonial states and scholars to recognise and govern communities labeled as “Dayak.” Decolonisation, therefore, ended formal political domination but did not uproot the conceptual structures of the epistemic system that had long been established.

Michel Foucault’s formulation of ‘power/knowledge’ provides a compelling framework to understand how postcolonial states in Borneo adopted and maintained these epistemic continuities. Foucault (1977) argues that power does not just regulate; it produces knowledge, and through that process produces the subjects that such knowledge then claims to describe. “Power and knowledge directly imply one another,” and there is no relation of power without the field of knowledge that accompanies it.18 Within this framework, colonial formulations adopted in postcolonial Borneo were not merely remnants of colonial terminology but became–or were reshaped into–elements of a continuing ‘regime of truth.’ This is a set of institutional apparatuses and discourses that authorise specific ways of classifying and organising interior populations in Borneo. As Foucault (1980) emphasises, “truth” is produced within institutional apparatuses–scientific, bureaucratic, and educational–that hold the authority to determine what counts as knowledge and who is entitled to articulate it.19 Thus, postcolonial states in Borneo did not only inherit colonial vocabularies; they also inherited the mechanisms that allowed these classifications to be executed administratively.

This continuity is an example of what Aníbal Quijano describes as the ‘coloniality of power,’ a concept that explains how colonial structures persist in postcolonial societies. According to Quijano (2000), the end of formal colonial domination does not eliminate the classificatory logic that colonial administrations used to organise populations; rather, these logics are reorganised and redeployed in new institutional settings. One of the “fundamental axes” of modern power is colonial social classification through racial hierarchy–an axis that, he argues, is “more durable and stable than the colonialism in which it was first constituted.”20 Within this framework, postcolonial states in Borneo inherited not only territorial sovereignty but also the Eurocentric epistemologies that determine which identities are knowable, which differences are governable, and which forms of knowledge are legitimate. While some postcolonial scholars appeared objective or sympathetic in describing Borneo's interior peoples, their efforts did not disrupt the deep-rooted epistemic frameworks in place. Wallace, for instance, in The Malay Archipelago (1890), described the Dyaks as having an “undoubtedly high” moral character, though he acknowledged that this might surprise readers accustomed to stories of “headhunters and pirates.”21 Similarly, Nieuwenhuis in “In Centraal Borneo” (1894-1900) tried to counter depictions of the Dayak as violent by emphasising their “gentleness and peacefulness.”22 A century later, Sellato (2002) also recognised the vast diversity of communities grouped under “Dayak,” noting that they “defy any attempt at being forced into a single label.”23 However, despite the credibility of these writings, such sympathetic portrayals did not automatically unsettle the long-standing epistemic structures framing Borneo’s interior populations.

In the postcolonial period, epistemic continuity emerged through vocabularies that seemed softer and more neutral but essentially rearticulated colonial classificatory logic. Terms such as “inlander,” “primitive,” “uncivilized,” “exotic,” and “animistic” were gradually replaced by “local community,” “traditional,” “authentic,” “empowered,” and “local knowledge”–words that seem progressive yet carry underlying assumptions about civilisational hierarchy and stages of development. This discursive shift became the basis through which postcolonial states defined and reorganised the communities grouped under the label "Dayak.”

The term later shifted to “Masyarakat Terasing” in 1978 and “Komunitas Adat Terpencil” in 1999.24 Nonetheless, these terms preserved the genealogical structure of the colonial categories such as “Inlandse Volken” and “Primitieve Stammen”–found in documents like the “Koloniaal Verslag,” “Dutch administrative reports,” and the “Indische Staatsregeling.” Implicitly, this translated colonial notions of “civilisational advancement” directly into the developmental idiom of postcolonial Indonesia.

Colonial epistemic continuity was evident in state institutions and academic fields as well, particularly anthropology. Postcolonial anthropology continued to shape how “Dayak” came to be known. As King (2017) notes, Anthropologists in postcolonial Borneo closely tied the development of anthropology to colonial administrative interests., such as research under the Colonial Social Science Research Council (CSSRC), which reinforced classificatory frameworks well into the post-independence period.27 Zawawi (2017) similarly observes that Geddes’s “The Land Dayaks of Sarawak” (1954) still prioritised “remoteness” and “native exoticism,” depicting the Bidayuh as “shy and withdrawn”—a trope consistent with Brooke-era imaginaries. In Foucault’s (1977) terms, these administrative and academic formations recreated a ‘regime of truth’ that defined legitimate knowledge about the Dayak and their treatment. Indonesia’s translation of Inlandse Volken into “Suku Terasing,” Malaysia’s constitutional codification of “natives of Sabah and Sarawak,” and Brunei’s absorption of Indigenous diversity into state-defined Malay each reveal that postcolonial Bornean states did not merely inherit colonial terminology; they preserved the mechanisms that allowed such classifications to continue operating. In line with what Quijano (2000) describes as the “coloniality of power,” the independence of former colonies did not sever colonial classificatory logics but reorganised them through new bureaucratic vocabularies. Terms like “Suku Terasing,” “native of Sabah and Sarawak,” or “indigenous of the Malay race” explicitly preserved classificatory distinctions while obscuring their genealogical origins. Dayak communities themselves did not retain the authority to define what “Dayak” means, how it should be classified, and who may speak on its behalf. This hegemony circulated through ministries, constitutions, and academic canons, meaning that what endured was not just the softened label but the entire epistemic framework of colonialism underlying its etymology. It is this epistemic continuity–embedded in administrative, legal, and developmental apparatuses–that has made many postcolonial policies toward interior communities labeled “Dayak” neither neutral nor proportional, and often discriminatory and marginalising. The Governance of Difference: Institutionalising the “Dayak” in Postcolonial States ​ In the postcolonial states of Borneo, colonial ideas were not just translated into bureaucratic language; they became the foundation of institutional policies. In Indonesia, Malaysia, and Brunei Darussalam, the epistemic logic embedded in the label “Dayak” seemed to grant state apparatuses the authority to regulate, supervise, or even intervene in the lives of interior populations. Government authorities treated “Dayak” as a population to be “developed,” relocated, redistributed, or culturally standardized according to the logic of modern state formation. Rather than dismantling the hierarchical concepts inherited from colonialism, postcolonial regimes translated them into routine administrative practices–social welfare programs, affirmative action policies, territorial management, and cultural regulation–that framed the Dayak as subjects to be governed within new national orders.

To understand how postcolonial states managed ‘Dayak’ communities in Borneo, Michel Foucault’s (1991) concept of governmentality is insightful. It defines government as the ‘conduct of conduct,’ a rationality that determines governance structures and their impact on specific populations like the Dayak. Governmentality highlights how modern states shape populations through administrative routines, classificatory schemes, and normative interventions—not only through coercion. Alongside this, James C. Scott's (1998) formulation of legibility explains how states attempt to simplify complex social worlds into standardized, readable forms—through mapping, enumeration, categorization, and spatial organization—so that they can be monitored and acted upon. Together, these frameworks

show that postcolonial states did not merely inherit the category “Dayak,” but actively reproduced it through disproportionate governmental techniques.

The disproportionate governance of ‘Dayak’ communities in Indonesia became particularly evident during “Orde Baru” (“Suharto’s New Order regime”). The developmental apparatus of the state positioned the Dayak as a population requiring intervention and supervision. Through “UU No. 6 Tahun 1974” on Social Welfare, the Ministry of Social Affairs launched the “Isolated Tribes Development Program” in the 1970s. This programme framed Dayak communities as “primitive,” “isolated,” and “backward,” seeking to “educate,” “relocate,” and “modernise” them to become “productive” citizens aligned with the national development agenda. As Mangenda (2024) notes, one of its main attributes was the “integrated resettlement” scheme (“Permukiman Terintegrasi”), under which the government carried out large-scale relocations of Dayak communities living in longhouses. Authorities deemed these longhouses–central to communal life and foundational to their cultural world–“unfit,” “unhygienic,” and “pre-modern.” As a result, communities were moved to new settlements and placed in individual housing units–a spatial configuration very different from their traditional communal living patterns.29 Parallel to this relocation, through “UU No. 5 Tahun 1967” on Forestry, the state expanded its authority over forest areas that had long been customary domains of Dayak communities. By categorising large tracts of customary forest as state forest, this restructuring led to land dispossession, weakened customary territorial rights, and produced long-standing agrarian conflicts that still affect many Dayak communities today.

Following the discussion of Indonesia, Malaysia's approach to postcolonial governance also reveals significant insights, particularly with the introduction of the New Economic Policy (NEP) in 1971, after the 1969 racial riots. As Lee (2022) shows, the NEP fundamentally reorganised Malaysia’s political economy by institutionalising affirmative policies “Pro-Bumiputera”, a category that includes Malays (56%) and non-Malay indigenous groups in Sabah and Sarawak (14%). Although inclusive, its implementation produced unequal benefits.30 He also notes that public opinion on the NEP remains “polarised and deadlocked,” reflecting long-standing tensions over the concentration of benefits within Malay-dominated sectors.31 Practically, the NEP cemented an “internal hierarchy” within the Bumiputera category: Malays became the primary beneficiaries of redistributive policies, while non-Malay Indigenous groups occupied subordinate positions. In Brunei, postcolonial state formation took a different direction compared to Indonesia and Malaysia. After independence in 1984, the consolidation of the national ideology Melayu Islam Beraja (MIB) had significant cultural effects. Constitutionally, the state absorbed the diversity of interior populations and merged them under a state-defined Malay identity. As Kumpoh (2023) notes, MIB functions as a “homogenising agent,” shaping cultural policy, education, and public representation in ways that narrow the visibility of non-Malay Indigenous identities.33 Among the Dusun, this pressure appears in everyday practices: a declining use of the Dusun language among younger generations, replacement of traditional attire with Malay-Islamic dress in public settings, and the shrinking of customary rituals. At the institutional level, Kershaw (1998) shows that defining “Malay” as a cultural–religious category and reserving top political offices for Malay Muslims has effectively excluded non-Muslim Indigenous groups, such as the Dusun and Murut, from full political incorporation.34 These developments reveal how Brunei's postcolonial governance reshaped the cultural and political visibility of interior populations through mechanisms that emphasized assimilation into a state-scripted Malay identity.

Taken together, the governance of Dayak difference in Indonesia, Malaysia, and Brunei shows how postcolonial states translated colonial classificatory logics into concrete governmental mechanisms. Referring to Foucault’s (1991) concept of governmentality, these policies not only regulated the “Dayak” but also shaped and normalised them through administrative routines like resettlement schemes, welfare categories, constitutional classifications, and cultural standardisation. These techniques positioned Dayak communities as dependent subjects but as populations whose behaviour, mobility, and cultural life had to be engineered to fit national developmental and ideological projects.

Through James C. Scott’s (1998) concept of legibility, the policy dynamics in postcolonial Borneo also show how states reduced the sociocultural complexity of Dayak communities into simplified administrative forms. In Indonesia, the shift to “Suku Terasing” made interior populations legible through relocation grids and mapped forests; in Malaysia, the “Bumiputera” framework, which standardised indigeneity into a redistributive hierarchy, and the MIB doctrine in Brunei, which compressed cultural plurality into a state-defined Malay template. Across all three settings, Governing difference did not dismantle colonial classification but reproduced it through developmental, redistributive, and homogenising rationalities aimed at making Indigenous populations visible, measurable, and subject to state intervention.

Ultimately, the governance of Dayak difference in postcolonial Borneo crystallised into three distinct yet structurally related modes. In Indonesia, governmentality took the form of “assimilationist developmentalism,” where integrated resettlement schemes under the “Pembinaan Suku Terasing” programme sought to reshape Dayak communities into “primitive” yet modernisable subjects. In Malaysia, the government exercised state power through a “redistributive hierarchy” institutionalised under the NEP, formally including “Dayak” groups while subordinating them beneath Malay hegemony. In Brunei, governance moved toward “cultural homogenisation,” where MIB and the Nationality Act absorbed the diversity of interior populations into an administratively defined Malayness—rendering their cultures marginal or invisible in public space. These heavy modes of governance created conditions for the rearticulation and renegotiation of Dayak identity in contemporary Borneo. Rearticulating the “Dayak”: Negotiation, Hybridity, and Cultural Reclaiming The rearticulation of “Dayak” in postcolonial Borneo involves communities actively negotiating their cultural identity amidst the evolving political landscapes of Indonesia, Malaysia, and Brunei. Thus, the contemporary reactivation of “Dayak” in Borneo does not lead to a single or uniform expression of identity. Postcolonial governance pushes Dayak actors to navigate, reinterpret, and creatively reclaim the categories they negotiate–whether through cultural revival, political mobilisation, or everyday practices of identity. These negotiations lead to hybrid forms: in Indonesia, Dayak identity consolidates; in Malaysia, it fragments along ethnolinguistic lines; and in Brunei, it persists in private cultural practices.

The reactivation of “Dayak” identity can be understood through Homi K. Bhabha's (1994) concept of hybridity, which emphasizes that cultural identity is formed through the reshaping of dominant symbols and discourses, rather than through the preservation of an unchanging origin. Rather than treating identity as a stable essence, this approach highlights how Dayak subjectivity emerges through processes of negotiation and partial appropriation of the categories imposed by the state.35 Hybridity operates in what Bhabha calls the “third space”–an in-between zone where cultural forces that do not always align–such as state classifications and customary norms–meet and tension one another, allowing new and unexpected identities to emerge. This process unsettles dichotomies such as “traditional vs modern” or “inside vs outside,” showing identity as something produced through ongoing negotiation rather than static inheritance.

In line with this, Stuart Hall (1990) argues that cultural identity is always evolving and never complete from the outset. Identity is always in the process of “becoming”–shaped by history, context, and power relations.36 It is rooted in a dynamic historical experience, continually reshaped through shifts in conditions and the ways individuals are positioned within competing cultural narratives. In the case of “Dayak” in Borneo, these processes unfold within very different political configurations, cultural regimes, everyday conditions, and forms of local agency, resulting in multiple hybrid formations rather than a single shared form.

In Indonesia, the negotiation of “Dayak” identity is evident in the growing acceptance of “Dayak” as a racial and cultural category, though this acceptance varies among sub-ethnic groups with distinct languages and traditions. This consolidation can be seen in local knowledge projects such as Tjilik Riwut’s “Kalimantan Membangun” (1979), which grouped the Dayak into seven major clusters comprising 405 subgroups. Additionally, “Mozaik Dayak” (2004), published by Institut Dayakologi, documented 156 languages of the “Dayak Sub-Groups” in West Kalimantan. These knowledge productions establish “Dayak” as the primary domain of identification, while local identities such as “Iban,” “Bidayuh,” “Kanayatan,” “Taman,” and others are positioned as derivative domains. This epistemic rearticulation extends beyond academic writing and becomes rooted in everyday social life through self-identification practices across Dayak communities in Indonesian.

The consolidation of Dayak identity in Indonesia was also strengthened by the establishment of the “Dewan Adat Dayak (DAD)” (“Dayak Customary Council”) in 2001 and the “Majelis Adat Dayak Nasional (MADN)” (“National Dayak Customary Council”) in 2008, which function as hybrid political–cultural spaces where representatives of various Dayak subgroups negotiate collective narratives for political mobilisation. One outcome of this consolidation is large-scale cultural events such as “Gawai Dayak,” which have become performative arenas where Dayak actors assert and reformulate their collective identity in the national sphere. At the same time, Dayak youth activism–especially in social media—through various forms of digital content has helped shift representations of the Dayak away from colonial stereotypes of “primitiveness” toward portrayals as ecological guardians, cultural bearers, and Indigenous groups proud of their identity. Through interconnected forms of knowledge production, institutional consolidation, cultural performativity, and everyday negotiation, “Dayak” in Indonesia is consolidated under a single umbrella yet appears as a dynamic hybrid identity continually reshaped within modern social and political landscapes.

In contrast, unlike Indonesia, where “Dayak” has become a consolidated racial and political–cultural category, in Malaysia–although several cultural and political associations use the term–the consolidation is more fragmented. Dayak communities are more often organised along ethnolinguistic lines such as “Iban,” “Bidayuh,” and various “Orang Ulu” groups. This pattern can be seen in cultural and political consolidation processes such as the 36 Stuart Hall, “Cultural Identity and Diaspora,” in Identity: Community, Culture, Difference, ed. Jonathan Rutherford (London: Lawrence & Wishart, 1990), 225–226.

formation of the “Dayak Bidayuh National Association” in 1955 and the “Sarawak Dayak Iban Association” (SADIA) in 1984, which mobilise the label “Dayak” strategically while still emphasising the distinct histories and cultural boundaries of each community. This fragmentation is visible in practical politics, as Hazis (2012) notes, where elite divisions—especially Iban leadership dominance–have contributed to leadership crises in the “Sarawak National Party (SNAP)” and “Parti Bansa Dayak Sarawak (PBDS),” hindering a united Dayak political platform.37

However, in the field of activism, an important arena for negotiating Dayak identity in Malaysia emerges in struggles over “Native Customary Rights (NCR).” Among the Iban, Bidayuh, and Orang Ulu, NCR cases have combined legal claims with territorial history and customary norms to assert their position as rightful owners of customary land. NCR proceedings serve as legal disputes and identity negotiation spaces, where litigants present genealogical testimony and histories of forest use–”pemakai menoa”–recognized by the Court of Appeal in Sarawak. As Bulan (1998) notes, the courts affirmed that the Iban concept of “pemakai menoa” indeed exists–a significant judicial recognition that allowed customary territorial principles to enter the logic of NCR litigation.38 Through these hybrid practices–located between customary law and state law–“Dayak” in Malaysia is rearticulated as a legal–territorial and moral identity grounded in land claims, historical continuity, and political agency within struggles for NCR recognition.

In contrast to Indonesia and Malaysia, Brunei has absorbed various interior groups, often classified as “Dayak” in regional ethnography, into the official Malay category through “Puak-Puak Melayu.” As a result, the articulation of “Dayak” among the Belait, Bisayah, Brunei, Dusun, Kedayan, Murut, and Tutong rarely appears in public or formal political spaces. Instead, such articulations persist in domestic everyday practices where genealogical memory, family rituals, and small cultural practices maintain Indigenous identity outside public visibility. As Kumpoh (2023) observes, The coexistence of Malayness in public and indigeneity in private is evident in several practices, such as Dusun women adopting Malay–Islamic dress in public, blending customary elements into wedding and funeral rituals, transmitting ethnic languages through interactions with elders and village return visits, and adapting rituals, such as grave cleaning, to resemble Malay–Islamic practices.39 These developments occur partly due to state homogenisation strategies that effectively erode fragile indigenous identities. When viewed across countries, the negotiation of Dayak identity creates hybrid formations shaped by the political and epistemic configurations of each postcolonial state. In Indonesia, public consolidation rearticulates as “Dayak,” becoming a shared cultural domain that unites many subgroups through scholarship, institutions, festivals, and new media. In Malaysia, rearticulation takes the form of fragmentation, as the identities of Iban, Bidayuh, and Orang Ulu remain rooted in different ethnolinguistic and political trajectories; yet hybridity also appears through legal-territorial mobilisation in NCR struggles. In Brunei, rearticulation appears as latent hybridity, sustained through domestic, kinship-based, and cultural memory practices beneath the surface of Malay administrative identity. These trajectories illustrate that the rearticulation of “Dayak” is a complex, context-dependent process, highlighting the diverse ways communities navigate identity within postcolonial power structures.

Through Bhabha's lens of hybridity, this process shows how Dayak actors occupy multiple “third spaces”: the public consolidation space in Indonesia, the fragmented legal-territorial arena in Malaysia, and the intimate domestic sphere in Brunei. These spaces mediate identity and generate new forms of subjectivity that unsettle essentialist dichotomies like “modern vs traditional” or “state vs culture.” Hall’s idea of identity as an ongoing process further clarifies that these formations emerge from historical negotiations rather than fixed origins–each shaped by state ideologies, classificatory regimes, and everyday realities. Ultimately, what emerges is not a single transnational Dayak identity, but a constellation of hybrid identities–consolidated, fragmented, or latent. Together, these communities in postcolonial Borneo reclaim and reimagine their indigeneity amidst unequal power in Indonesia, Malaysia, and Brunei Darussalam. Conclusion This paper reveals that the term “Dayak” is a powerful colonial construct that continues to influence the perception and governance of Indigenous identity in Borneo. By tracing the genealogy of the term, the paper demonstrates that it was never simply a classificatory category; from the outset, colonial authorities used it as a mechanism to construct the “interior” as a domain of difference requiring interpretation, discipline, and intervention. It is essential to note that this classificatory logic did not disappear with decolonisation. Instead, it transformed into a form of state rationality that continues to determine how the Dayak appear as political subjects.

Building on the persistence of colonial logic, a comparative study of Indonesia, Malaysia, and Brunei reveals how postcolonial regimes reworked these epistemologies into distinct governance strategies. Indonesia revived colonial narratives of primitiveness through “assimilationist developmentalism”; Malaysia translated colonial racial taxonomy into a “redistributive hierarchy” that institutionalised unequal forms of indigeneity; while Brunei pursued “cultural homogenisation” that removed non-Malay Indigenous identities from the

public sphere. Despite their differences, all three share a deeper continuity: the Dayak are treated not as producers of knowledge but as objects of legibility—populations to be mapped, classified, corrected, and absorbed into national projects.

Despite the enduring influence of these epistemic structures, they do not negate Dayak agency. Rather, this very dynamic creates the conditions through which Dayak actors negotiate, reinterpret, and reshape the categories imposed upon them. Contemporary articulations of Dayak identity emerge not outside colonial and postcolonial classifications, but through appropriation, inversion, and strategic redeployment. These negotiations unfold in spaces of cultural revitalisation, political mobilisation, and everyday practices of language and ritual. At the same time, they reveal that identity is a fluid concept, continuously evolving within unequal relations of power.

Ultimately, this paper contends that “Dayak” represents an epistemic battleground where colonial legacies, state power, and Indigenous rearticulation converge, offering profound insights into the dynamics of identity and power in Borneo. Viewing “Dayak” as a formation that is constructed and subsequently reclaimed–rather than a stable identity–offers a more conceptually accurate and politically attentive theoretical model for comprehending indigeneity in Borneo. This approach emphasizes the urgency of questioning the classificatory regimes of postcolonial states that manage differences long after colonialism has ended. References
